# Image Sourcing — a distinct, subject-matched, high-impact picture on EVERY slide

Every brief ships with the actual images it should use, sourced from the web, LOOKED AT, scored, and
embedded so they render in the brief and Step 2 can pull them straight in. No "find a photo later."
The image is the FOCUS of each slide; the headline is a passenger on it.

This file covers HOW to find images. `references/image-qa.md` is the GATE that decides whether a found
image is allowed to ship. Both are mandatory. An image that was never scored in `image-qa.md` is not
approved, no matter how good it looked in the search results.

---

## What to produce

For each idea: 1 hero image (static) or **one DISTINCT background image per slide** (carousel: hook +
every body slide + CTA). An image may serve at most 2 slides, and only as visibly different crops.
Step 2 renders no flat or gradient cards, so a missing or repeated slide image is a blocking gap.

Each shipped image carries:

- an **inline embed** so the photo actually renders in the brief,
- a **direct image URL** (the real file, not a search page, not a thumbnail),
- **source page, author/credit, and license**,
- its **`image-qa.md` scorecard**, written into the brief.

---

## The order of operations is not negotiable

The single biggest cause of generic imagery is filtering by license before filtering by impact. A
Creative-Commons-first search returns the Commons / Unsplash / Wikipedia pool, which is precisely where
dated, posed, over-used photography lives. Impact loses every time because it is only allowed to choose
from what survived the wrong filter.

**Always run in this order:**

1. **SUBJECT** — what is this slide literally about? (Rule #1 below)
2. **IMPACT** — of the images that show that subject, which one stops a scroll? (the shock bar)
3. **LEGIBILITY** — of those, which gives the headline and eyebrow room? (the zone check)
4. **RIGHTS** — only now, resolve the licensing for the image you actually want. (the rights ladder)

If the winning image cannot be cleared for publication, do NOT fall back to a weak-but-free photo.
Work the rights ladder: find the same moment on Commons, find an official press-kit frame of the same
subject, or flag the brief `INTERNAL MOCKUP ONLY` and say so explicitly. A free generic photo is a worse
outcome than a flagged strong one, because the flagged one can still be licensed or re-shot.

---

## Rule #1: match the SUBJECT — a metaphor is the exception, not the default

Before anything else, ask: is this slide's claim ABOUT a real, named thing — a company, a product, a
tool, a platform, a person, a specific event? If yes, the image must show THAT thing, or something
directly and literally from its world. Not a metaphor borrowed from an unrelated industry.

**The five-unrelated-posts test.** Could this exact photo illustrate five other, completely unrelated
posts without looking out of place? If yes, it is too generic. Go find the one that is unmistakably
THIS story. Write the answer to this test into the scorecard for every slide — it is a scored parameter
in `image-qa.md`, not a rhetorical question.

### Worked examples

| Slide's claim | Generic pick (REJECT) | Subject-matched pick (SHIP) |
|---|---|---|
| "Meta's AI now writes the ad for you" | Robot arm in a car factory | A real Meta Advantage+ / Ads Manager interface frame, or Zuckerberg on stage at the actual keynote |
| "Every DTC ad looks identical now" | Wine bottles on a bottling line | A grid screenshot of actual near-identical DTC ads from the Meta Ad Library |
| "Ferrari builds fewer cars than people want" | Generic luxury sports car on a road | The Maranello line itself, or a Ferrari unveiling with the crowd pressed against the barrier |
| "Nike bet the brand on one athlete" | Running shoes on white background | The actual campaign still, or the athlete mid-moment in the ad |
| "Senna drove differently in the rain" | Rain on a windscreen | Senna's car throwing spray at Donington, visor up, real race frame |
| "Ogilvy wrote headlines like an engineer" | Vintage typewriter, dramatic light | Ogilvy himself at his desk, or the actual Rolls-Royce ad artwork |

Notice the pattern: the rejected column is always *technically on-theme in the abstract* and *visually
disconnected from what the post is actually about*. That is exactly the failure mode.

### When a metaphor IS warranted

Only when the slide's claim is genuinely abstract with no real subject to photograph — a mindset idea,
a timeless principle, a feeling with no company or product attached. Even then, prefer a metaphor drawn
from the post's own world (marketing, tech, agency life, the anchor's own industry) over one borrowed
from a completely different field. Then, and only then, use the beat→query method in the Appendix.

---

## The shock bar (step 2 of the order)

Once the subject is right, pick for impact. Every image must clear ALL of these:

- **Charged, not posed.** Emotion and tension: a face mid-emotion, direct eye contact, violent or
  raking light, an extreme close-up, an absurd juxtaposition, a scale contrast, a provocative or
  polarizing frame. The scream, not the headshot. The storm over the building, not the facade.
- **Current-feeling.** Prefer recent, contemporary, high-resolution imagery. For a historical anchor,
  find the highest-resolution, most striking frame that exists — colour over grainy black and white
  when a good colour frame is available. If your first source only turns up something dated or
  over-used, that is a FAIL, not a compromise. Escalate to another source.
- **High resolution.** At least 1600px on the short side, enough to fill 1080x1350 without softening.
  Skip thumbnails. A beautiful 600px image is a rejected image.
- **Distinct per slide.** The carousel must never look like one photo re-tinted eight times.

The hero must be able to stop a scroll on its own, before the headline is even read. If it cannot, it
is not the hero.

---

## Banned tropes — automatic rejection

These are rejected on sight, regardless of resolution or licence. If a search returns mostly these, the
query is wrong; narrow it rather than settling.

- Business handshake, any handshake
- Laptop on a desk; hands on a keyboard; a mug beside a notebook
- Generic open-plan office; a meeting around a table; sticky notes on glass
- Person in a suit pointing at a rising chart
- Abstract glowing network lines, circuit boards, blue "digital transformation" wallpaper
- Humanoid robot or robot arm used as a stand-in for "AI"
- Night city skyline with light trails
- Chess pieces used for "strategy"; a lightbulb used for "idea"; a rocket used for "growth"
- Stock-model "diverse team high-fiving"
- A lone figure on a mountain summit
- Any photo whose subject is clearly a paid model performing an emotion

## Where to look (in the order Rule #1 dictates)

### 1. Google News / Google Images — FIRST STOP for any real named subject

This is the default starting point, not a fallback. Whenever a slide's claim is about a real, named
subject (a company's product launch, a specific tool, a stated figure, an event), search here before
touching Unsplash or Pexels. A stock library cannot show you the actual thing the post is about.

1. Search the subject AND the specific moment — `Meta Advantage+ dashboard`, `Mark Zuckerberg Connect
   keynote stage`, not `advertising`. Add the product or platform name alone to widen the net.
2. **Do NOT apply the usage-rights filter at this stage.** Find the right image first; licensing is
   step 4. Filtering to Creative Commons here is what produces the generic result.
3. Click through to the ORIGINAL source page — company newsroom, official press kit, a Commons mirror.
   Never take Google's cached thumbnail as the final URL. Pull the direct file URL, the credit, and the
   licence from THAT page.
4. Wire-service photography (AP, Reuters, Getty, bylined news-org staff) is almost never free for
   commercial reuse. Note it and work the rights ladder below rather than silently shipping it.

### 2. The subject's own sources

Often better than any search: the company newsroom and press kit, the official brand media library, the
product's own site and interface, the athlete's or team's official media, the museum or archive that
holds the historical material. These are both the most subject-matched AND the most likely to be
cleared for editorial use.

### 3. Pinterest — a lead, never a source

Two legitimate uses: spotting a current aesthetic or composition trend, and searching the exact
subject, product, or company name to see how it is actually being shown in the wild (product shots,
interface captures, campaign stills) that a plain stock search would never surface.

Every Pinterest image is re-pinned from somewhere else and its listed source is often wrong, stripped,
or lost. Use it to find the LOOK or the SUBJECT, then click through to the original and take the direct
URL, author, and licence from there. **Never embed a `pinimg.com` URL in a brief.**

### 4. Wikimedia Commons — real people, historical and editorial subjects

1. Find the file with web search (e.g. `Ayrton Senna site:commons.wikimedia.org File`). Prefer the
   highest-resolution version available.
2. Build the direct URL with FilePath (spaces become underscores):
   `https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/<File_Name>.jpg`
3. Record the licence (CC BY, CC BY-SA, public domain) and the author. Credit both.

Commons is deep but slow to catch up — the newest photo of a public figure or a very recent event often
is not uploaded yet. For a this-year news moment, Google News is the better first stop; come back to
Commons for the highest-resolution archival frame once you know what you are looking for.

### 5. Unsplash / Pexels — the metaphor case only

Once Rule #1 has confirmed that a cross-domain metaphor is genuinely warranted, or for deliberately
non-specific context that is not meant to depict anything real, these are the best free, high-resolution,
rights-clear libraries. Use the direct CDN image URL. Record the photographer and page. Still credit.

Never open these first. Reaching for Unsplash before establishing the subject is the single most common
route to a generic brief.

## Look at the image before you choose it

A URL that resolves is not a photo you have seen. Choosing from filenames, alt text, and search-result
captions is exactly how a pick ends up technically on-theme and visually dead.

**For every candidate you intend to ship, you must actually view the frame and write one line
describing what is literally in it** — the subject, the framing, where the negative space sits. That
sentence goes into the scorecard. If you cannot see the image, you cannot approve it; find one you can.

The sandbox shell cannot reach image hosts (the network allowlist blocks them), so do not try to
download candidates with bash. View them through the connected Chrome browser, or have the file placed
into the working folder and read it from there.

---

## Bring a candidate set, not a single result

One search producing one pick is not a choice, it is an accident. For every slide:

- Gather **3 to 5 candidates** that pass Rule #1.
- Score each against `references/image-qa.md`.
- Ship the winner, and record in the brief **one line on why it beat the runner-up**.

If fewer than 3 candidates clear Rule #1, the query is too narrow or the slide's claim is too vague.
Fix the query, or sharpen the slide's claim so it has a real subject to photograph.

---

## Pick for legibility too (step 3 of the order)

Drama that cannot carry text is a wasted pick. When candidates tie on subject match AND the shock bar,
prefer the one that gives the design step room:

- A clear area of negative space (sky, shadow, a wall, dark clothing, an out-of-focus background) where
  the bottom-anchored serif headline can sit.
- **Check the eyebrow/kicker zone just above the headline too.** A face or a logo sitting exactly where
  the eyebrow lands (low on a subject's neck or collar) is a bad pick even when the headline zone below
  it is clean. Check both zones.
- Enough tonal range to hold contrast once the navy scrim or duotone goes over it.
- A subject not so close to the frame edge that a safe crop (~80px on 1080x1350) cuts the focal point.

Never trade subject match away for legibility. A generic-but-legible photo is still the wrong photo.
This is a selection tip only — Step 2 owns the actual contrast, scrim, and crop, and prints a measured
`[contrast]` reading per zone so a bad pick is caught at render time as well as by eye.

---

## Rights ladder (step 4 of the order)

Resolve rights for the image you actually want, in this order. Stop at the first rung that clears.

1. **Officially cleared** — company newsroom or press kit, official brand media library, an image
   Rarity owns or has licensed.
2. **Commons / CC** — the same subject or moment hosted on Wikimedia Commons or under a CC licence.
   Fine to publish **with correct attribution**; record the exact licence and required credit.
3. **Free stock** — Unsplash or Pexels, for the metaphor and generic-context case only.
4. **Flag it** — if the strongest image is wire-service or otherwise unlicensed, mark it clearly in the
   brief as `INTERNAL MOCKUP ONLY — not cleared for publication`, and note what a cleared substitute
   would be. Never quietly swap in a weak free photo to dodge this conversation.

Images found through Google or traced through Pinterest carry the SAME bar: credit and licence come
from the original source page you traced to, never from the search listing.

---

## Verify before shipping

- Confirm each direct URL genuinely resolves to the image, through Chrome — not through bash.
- Confirm you are shipping the TRACED original-source URL, never a search result or a `pinimg.com` link.
- Confirm the file is at least 1600px on the short side.
- If you cannot confirm a clean, working direct URL, choose another image. A dead link becomes a missing
  slide, because Step 2's downloads run unattended.

---

## Embed it in the brief

- Inline, so the photo renders: `![<short desc> — <author>, <license>](<direct-url>)`
- Plus the IMAGES table, **one row per slide**: slide · description · direct URL · source page · author ·
  licence · QA score.

---

## Handoff to Step 2

`rarity-ig-designer` renders everything itself in Python/Pillow — no Canva, no Figma. It reads each
slide's direct image URL from the IMAGES table and downloads it into
`public/Instagram Briefings/Designs <Month>/<Idea N - Name>/` (hero.jpg for the hook, bg2.jpg … bgN.jpg for the
rest), then composites the duotone and scrim treatment on top. Keep every direct URL exact, distinct,
and confirmed working.

## Appendix: the beat→query method (metaphor case ONLY)

Use this **only after Rule #1 has confirmed the slide has no real subject to photograph.** Reaching for
this table by default is the main reason briefs come back generic.

Don't search the topic, search the BEAT — what that specific line needs to FEEL like — in three to six
concrete words: subject + action/emotion + framing/era.

| Slide's beat | Weak query (too literal) | Stronger query (beat, high-impact) |
|---|---|---|
| Scarcity and waiting | luxury goods | queue outside closed shutter, dawn, cold |
| A generational shift | Gen Z workplace | young employee walking out an office door mid-stride |
| A collapse | company decline | empty storefront at dusk, signage half removed |
| Discipline and obsession | hard work | athlete alone training before sunrise, breath visible |
| A provocative claim | business success | crowd roaring in a stadium, faces contorted |
| Being ignored | communication | one person mid-shout in a silent crowd |

Steer toward the shock bar with words like "dramatic light", "close up", "intense", "cinematic",
"editorial", "high resolution". If the first query returns only flat stock, narrow it — add an emotion,
an era, a specific object — rather than settling.

Every image found this way still goes through `references/image-qa.md`. The metaphor route lowers the
subject-match requirement; it does not lower the impact bar.
