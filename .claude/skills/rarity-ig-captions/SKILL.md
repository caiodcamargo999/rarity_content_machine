# Rarity IG — Captions (Step 3 of 3)

This skill writes the **caption**, not the idea and not the art. It takes what Step 1
(`rarity-ig-idea-engine`) decoded and what Step 2 (`rarity-ig-designer`) already put on the image,
and writes the piece of copy that lives underneath the post: the thing someone reads on their phone
after the image already stopped their thumb.

**The headline stops the scroll. The caption decides whether the read continues, and whether the
post gets saved.** If the caption could have been posted by any marketing account with 10k
followers, it failed. It should read like it was typed by the one person at Rarity who cannot stop
thinking about this stuff and has a sharp, specific take, not a brand account reciting bullet points.
Not a press release. Not an AI that was told to "sound human" and overcorrected into forced slang or
fragmented text-speak. An actual, opinionated person who noticed something and had to say it.

That's the reference DNA this skill is built on: **@brandsdecoded__** captions do not re-explain the
carousel. They open on a confident, specific claim or fact, spend three real paragraphs proving it, and
add a beat the image didn't have room for — and they never carry a hashtag or an emoji. See
`references/caption-playbook.md` section 0 for three real, current captions pulled straight from the
account, with the assumptions they corrected in this skill's own earlier draft.

## What "good" looks like (read this first)

Seven things separate a Rarity caption from a caption-shaped wall of text:

1. **It never opens by restating the on-image headline or open loop subhead.** The image already said
   its line. If the caption's first sentence is a paraphrase of it, the reader gets nothing for
   stopping to read. The caption's job is to add a beat: the claim, the fact, the mechanism, the part
   that didn't fit on the slide. See `references/caption-playbook.md` section 1 for the eight opening
   techniques.
2. **It survives Instagram's truncation.** Feed view cuts the caption at roughly 125 characters
   (about two lines) before "... more." Whatever comes before that cut has to work standing alone, or
   nothing after it gets read. Write the opening, then check it in isolation.
3. **It sounds like one specific, opinionated person, not a brand account.** Confident claims stated
   as fact, not hedged. Contractions where they're natural in English. No corporate throat-clearing.
   Run every draft through the human voice test in `references/caption-playbook.md` section 4 before
   it ships.
4. **It is three tight, real paragraphs, not a wall of undifferentiated text and not fragmented
   text-message lines.** The real reference account writes genuine, well-built sentences grouped into
   three short paragraphs plus a CTA line. That's the default shape here too. See
   `references/caption-playbook.md` section 2.
5. **Zero hashtags. Zero emoji. Always, no exceptions.** Not "sparingly," not "one or two" — none.
   This is a hard rule from Caio, confirmed against the real reference account, which never uses
   either.
6. **It reads like this week, not like a generic template.** If the exact same caption could have run
   a year ago or could run five years from now unchanged, it isn't doing its job. Anchor it in
   something current: a real number from the brief, a real current platform or culture detail, a quick
   live check if the brief doesn't already carry one. See `references/caption-playbook.md` section 2's
   freshness check.
7. **It closes on save and share, with variety** (no button, no follow pitch, no "hope you enjoyed
   this"), or on the rare occasion paragraph three's final line already lands hard enough alone, no CTA
   at all. The exact CTA sentence should not repeat post after post. See the CTA bank in
   `references/caption-playbook.md` section 5.

If a draft fails any of these, rewrite before shipping.

## Workflow

### 1. Read the reference first
Before writing, skim `references/caption-playbook.md`, especially section 0. It has three real,
current captions pulled directly from @brandsdecoded__, the eight opening/pattern-interrupt
techniques with worked examples, the three-paragraph structure, the human voice test, the banned
constructions list, the CTA bank, and a full worked before/after example (Senna) that shows exactly
how much a caption should move past the brief's placeholder draft.

### 2. Intake — get the decode, not just the copy
Pull these from the Content Idea Brief (`public/Instagram Briefings/Ideas <Month>/Idea N - Name.docx`, or
pasted):
- **The real thing** (Real fact / Anchor), **the insight** (Big idea), **the takeaway** (Audience
  takeaway) — this is the actual material the caption is made of.
- **Pillar** and **Hook trigger** — tells you the register (Mindset & High Performance often earns a
  blunter, harder-hitting caption; Growth Marketing & Branding can carry more of a wink).
- What's actually **on the image** — the headline, the italic accent word, the open loop subhead, and
  (for a carousel) the slide lines. If `rarity-ig-designer` has already run, check the rendered PNGs
  or the render spec directly rather than trusting the brief's draft headline, in case it changed
  during design QA.
- Ignore the brief's own `## CAPTION` field as a final answer — treat it as a rough first pass at
  best, and note it still carries the old 3-hashtag allowance, which this skill overrides. This
  skill's output supersedes it.
- **A current detail, if the brief doesn't already have one.** If the anchor is evergreen (no live
  time-peg) and nothing in the brief ties it to something happening right now, a quick web check for a
  current number, tool, or platform behavior to ground paragraph one or two goes a long way — see the
  freshness check in `references/caption-playbook.md` section 2.

**If there's no brief** (Caio pastes a topic, an image, or asks for a caption standalone): work from
whatever real fact, insight, and takeaway you can establish. If the core anchor is missing entirely
(no real fact to decode), ask one direct question rather than inventing one — a caption with no real
anchor is exactly the generic content this whole system exists to avoid.

### 3. Pick ONE opening technique
Choose from the eight techniques in `references/caption-playbook.md` section 1, ordered there by how
often the real reference actually uses each one: Thesis/Sharp Claim, Blunt Fact Drop,
Continuation/Twist, Confession, Direct Address, Wrong Turn, Genuine Question, and the rare Single-Word
Punch. Pick the one that fits the specific fact you have, not the one you used last time — repeating
the same opening move across a batch is its own pattern the audience will clock. Write 2 to 3
opening-line candidates before settling; the first sentence is the highest-leverage sentence in the
whole caption.

### 4. Write the three paragraphs
Paragraph one opens with the chosen technique and grounds it in something concrete. Paragraph two
develops the mechanism or the turn. Paragraph three lands the takeaway as a genuine final sentence, not
a recap. Keep the decode's real substance, but give yourself the room a headline never has: direct
address, first person, and fully explained mechanisms are all fair game here even where the on-image
decode voice would avoid them (see `references/caption-playbook.md` section 3 for exactly what carries
over from the slide voice and what doesn't). Read section 4 (the human voice test) as you write, not
after.

### 5. Close: the CTA
Pick a save-and-share phrasing from the CTA bank (playbook section 5) or write a new one in the same
family. Directive, never grateful. Or, if paragraph three's last sentence already lands hard enough on
its own, skip the CTA entirely rather than tacking on a weaker one for the sake of having one. **No
hashtags, ever** — not 3, not 1, none.

### 6. EN, then ES, then PT — adaptation, not translation
Finish and lock the English caption first. For Spanish, rebuild the opening with the *same technique*,
not a literal translation of the English line — a Thesis/Sharp Claim in English should still be a
Thesis/Sharp Claim in Spanish, but the actual sentence should read like it was written by a Spanish
speaker. Use neutral, general Spanish (tú form) unless Caio names a specific regional market. Then do
the same for Portuguese: rebuild the same technique again, in **Brazilian Portuguese**, not a literal
translation of the English or Spanish line — the sentence should read like it was written by a Brazilian
writer for a Brazilian audience. Handily, the skill's own reference account (@brandsdecoded__, section 0
of the playbook) already writes in Brazilian Portuguese, so lean on its real cadence and vocabulary
choices as a live model for register, even though the actual sentence must still be built fresh from
this caption's own content, never copied from the reference's words. Keep all three versions the same
length and shape; if the Spanish or Portuguese runs noticeably longer, tighten it rather than letting
the rhythm sprawl.

### 7. Run the human voice test before shipping
Read all three captions out loud. For each: does it sound like a specific, opinionated writer, not
committee copy? Does the opening survive alone, cut at ~125 characters? Is there a banned construction
hiding in it? Could it have run unchanged a year ago or five years from now? Full checklist in
`references/caption-playbook.md` section 4. Fix anything that fails; don't ship a caption you had to
talk yourself into.

### 8. Deliver
- Show all three captions in chat (EN + ES + PT), plus the 1 to 2 runner-up opening lines you didn't
  use, so Caio can swap the hook without a rewrite.
- Save the final version as `Caption - <Short Name>.md` in whichever folder is more complete for that
  idea: `public/Instagram Briefings/Designs <Month>/<Idea N - Name>/` if the art has already been rendered
  (this is the normal case — captions usually follow the finished post), otherwise
  `public/Instagram Briefings/Ideas <Month>/` next to the brief. Use the brief's existing `<Month>` and
  `<Idea N - Name>` — don't invent new folder names.
- Note in chat that this supersedes the brief's draft `## CAPTION` field (the docx itself is not
  auto-updated; flag it if Caio wants that regenerated via `rarity-ig-idea-engine`).

### Batch
For a batch of posts, vary the opening technique and the CTA phrasing across the set the same way
`rarity-ig-idea-engine` rotates pillars — two captions in a row that both open with a Blunt Fact Drop,
or both close with "Save this. Send it to a founder who needs it," reads like a template even if each
one is individually fine.

## Output discipline
- **No hashtags. Zero, always.** Not 3, not 1, not "if it fits" — none, on any caption, in any
  language. This is a hard rule from Caio and it overrides the older hashtag allowance still sitting in
  `rarity-ig-idea-engine`'s brief template; this skill's caption is the final word.
- **No emoji. Zero, always.** Same hard rule, no exceptions.
- **No hyphens or dashes.** Same house rule as the headline: no em dash, en dash, or hyphen-as-pause
  in the posted caption. Rewrite with periods, commas, or colons, or a paragraph break. (The real
  reference account does use dashes occasionally; Rarity's own house style still avoids them, since
  this rule predates this skill and also governs the on-image headline.)
- **Never open on the image's exact headline or subhead line.** Cross-check against what
  `rarity-ig-designer` actually rendered.
- **Anchor every caption in something current.** No caption that could have run unchanged a year ago
  or five years from now. See the freshness check in `references/caption-playbook.md` section 2.
- **The close is always save + share energy, never a follow pitch, never "hope you enjoyed this,"** or
  no CTA at all when the last line of paragraph three already lands. Vary the exact sentence (see the
  CTA bank).
- **Length:** three real paragraphs plus (usually) a CTA line. Most captions land well under
  Instagram's 2,200 character cap, but "short" does not mean fragment-short: match the real reference's
  actual density (two to four sentences per paragraph) rather than padding or over-compressing.
- One real anchor per caption. Never invent a fact, number, or quote that isn't already verified in
  the brief.

## Relationship to the other skills
This is **Step 3**, downstream of `rarity-ig-idea-engine` (Step 1, the idea and the brief) and
`rarity-ig-designer` (Step 2, the rendered art). It can also run standalone against a topic or an
already-finished post. It does not edit the brief's `.docx` or the rendered PNGs — it produces the
caption as its own deliverable, meant to be pasted into Instagram alongside whatever Step 2 built. Note
for future maintenance: `rarity-ig-idea-engine`'s own brief template still allows up to 3 hashtags in
its draft `## CAPTION` field; this skill's zero-hashtag rule overrides that draft, but if Caio wants
the brief template itself updated to match, that's a change inside `rarity-ig-idea-engine`, not here.
