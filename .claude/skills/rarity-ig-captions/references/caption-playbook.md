## 0. Real examples from @brandsdecoded__ (read this first)

Three real, current captions pulled directly from the account (checked 2026-07-11), to replace
assumption with evidence. All three share the same shape: **zero hashtags, zero emoji, three tight
paragraphs of real prose** (not fragmented text-message lines), a strong first sentence, and substance
that could only have been written this year, not five years ago. They're in Portuguese, the account's
actual language; Rarity ships English, Spanish, and Portuguese, so study the technique, not the words —
**except for the Portuguese caption**, where this account's real, current Brazilian Portuguese is the
closest possible live model for register and cadence. Even so, never lift its actual sentences: Rarity's
Portuguese caption is still built fresh from that post's own content, the same way the English and
Spanish ones are.

### Example A — instagram.com/p/DTu-bDGDKxs (dated 2026-01-20)
> O discurso do criador como protagonista central da plataforma começa a perder força quando a lógica
> de distribuição muda sem aviso e sem pedagogia pública. O Instagram deixa de recompensar presença
> constante e passa a priorizar sinais de retenção, intenção e profundidade. Isso desloca o eixo do
> jogo de visibilidade para um território menos performático e mais funcional.
>
> A leitura mais honesta não é sobre punição, mas sobre eficiência sistêmica. Conteúdo que existe
> apenas para sustentar alcance se torna ruído, enquanto ativos que resolvem algo concreto ganham
> espaço. O algoritmo não busca creators melhores, busca conteúdos mais previsíveis em termos de
> resultado para a plataforma.
>
> A consequência é uma reconfiguração silenciosa do papel de quem produz. Criar deixa de ser um fim em
> si e passa a ser um meio subordinado a objetivos claros de negócio, produto ou autoridade. O criador
> não desaparece, mas perde o status de centro e vira infraestrutura.
>
> Comente "máquina" e aproveite o lote antes de virar.

English gloss: *The idea of the creator as the platform's central character starts losing power the
moment distribution logic shifts without warning or public explanation [...] The algorithm isn't
looking for better creators, it's looking for content that's more predictable for the platform's own
outcomes [...] The creator doesn't disappear. He just stops being the center and becomes
infrastructure.* Opens on a **Thesis / Sharp Claim** (technique 1 below). Closes on a comment-to-DM
CTA, not save/share.

### Example B — instagram.com/p/DWywSV3DW3J (dated 2026-04-06)
> Essa conta nasceu em janeiro de 2025 com zero seguidores. Em 14 meses, chegou a 277 mil, sem reels,
> sem aparecer dançando. O formato que fez isso acontecer foi o carrossel de tendência: um tipo de
> conteúdo que parte de algo que já está acontecendo no mundo e traduz pro universo do seu público.
>
> Enquanto todo mundo posta dica genérica que a IA cospe em 3 segundos, quem conecta cultura com nicho
> ganha alcance e autoridade ao mesmo tempo.
>
> Agora esse sistema inteiro roda dentro do Claude, sem Canva, sem Figma, sem instalar nada. Comenta
> CLAUDE que eu te mando os detalhes dessa oferta de lançamento direto na DM.

English gloss: *This account was born in January 2025 with zero followers. In 14 months it reached
277 thousand, without reels, without dancing on camera. The format that did it: the trend carousel,
content that starts from something already happening in the world and translates it into your
audience's world. While everyone posts the generic tip AI spits out in three seconds, whoever connects
culture to niche gets reach and authority at the same time.* Opens on a **Blunt Fact Drop** (technique
2 below): the exact number, the exact timeline. Also worth noting: it names AI-generic content as the
enemy, in public, which is itself a very current, very "this year" move.

### Example C — instagram.com/p/DaqSF58lG6f (dated 2026-07-11, posted same day this playbook was
checked against the account)
> Amar o que já existe virou quase um ato de resistência. Num ambiente feito para mostrar sempre o
> próximo desejo, perceber o que já está na sua frente ficou mais difícil.
>
> O feed treina a comparação: corpos, viagens, conquistas, rotinas e vidas que parecem mais completas.
> A insatisfação não surge do nada, ela é alimentada por um sistema que lucra quando você sente falta.
>
> O ponto não é parar de querer mais. É não deixar que o algoritmo defina o que ainda falta.
> Contentamento não é acomodação: é recuperar a atenção sobre aquilo que já sustenta a vida agora.

English gloss: *Loving what already exists has become almost an act of resistance [...] The feed
trains comparison: bodies, trips, achievements, routines, lives that look more complete. Dissatisfaction
doesn't come from nowhere, it's fed by a system that profits when you feel like something's missing
[...] Contentment isn't complacency, it's reclaiming attention on what already sustains your life right
now.* Another **Thesis / Sharp Claim** opening. No CTA at all: it closes cold, on the turn itself.

### What this corrects
An earlier draft of this playbook assumed the account writes in short, fragmented, text-message-style
lines. It doesn't. All three examples above are **three tight paragraphs of real, flowing prose**, each
paragraph two to four sentences, genuinely complex sentence structure, no hashtags, no emoji, no forced
casualness. What makes them human isn't fragment rhythm. It's that every sentence carries real content
and a real, confident point of view, the way a sharp columnist writes, not the way a text message
reads. Sections 1 through 3 below are built to match this.

**One deliberate difference Rarity keeps on purpose:** all three real examples close on a comment-bait
CTA ("Comente X") or no CTA at all, never "save and share." Rarity's save-and-share close (no button,
no follow pitch) is an existing house rule from Caio that predates this skill and also governs the CTA
slide on the art itself, so it stays as the default here unless Caio says otherwise. Worth knowing the
reference account does something different: the comment-to-DM CTA is a real, currently-working pattern,
not a hypothetical, if a specific Rarity campaign ever calls for it.

**On staying current:** notice that Example A is *about* a live platform-algorithm shift, and Example B
is *about* AI flooding the feed with generic content right now, in public, using the word "Claude." A
caption that could have been posted unchanged a year ago, or five years from now, is not doing this
job. See section 2's freshness note.

## 1. Eight opening techniques (pattern interrupts)

The image already did its job of stopping the scroll with a hook (see `rarity-ig-idea-engine`'s hook
doctrine: CONTRARIAN, AUTHORITY/ANTI-HERO, INNOVATIVE IDEA, APPARENT NONSENSE, EXTREME CURIOSITY).
Those five triggers are for the **headline**. The caption's opening sentence has a different job: it
has already got the stopped scroller's eye, and now it has to earn the tap on "... more." That means
the caption's first sentence should almost never be a restatement of the image's trigger line. It
should feel like the start of a real argument, not a recap.

Pick ONE per caption to open paragraph one. Rotate across a batch. Ordered roughly by how often the
real reference actually uses each move.

### 1. The Thesis / Sharp Claim (the most common real pattern — see Examples A and C)
Open with a confident, slightly contrarian claim about how something actually works, stated as fact,
not hedged. The rest of paragraph one grounds it; paragraphs two and three prove it.
> "The idea of the founder as the center of the brand stops working the moment the market starts
> rewarding proof over presence."
> "Loving what you already have has quietly become an act of resistance."
> Spanish: "La idea del fundador como centro de la marca deja de funcionar en el momento en que el
> mercado empieza a premiar la prueba por encima de la presencia."
> Portuguese: "A ideia do fundador como centro da marca para de funcionar no momento em que o mercado
> passa a premiar a prova em vez da presença."

### 2. The Blunt Fact Drop (see Example B)
Open on the sharpest number or fact, embedded in a real sentence, not floated alone.
> "This account had zero followers in January. Fourteen months later it has 277,000, and it never
> once used a reel."
> Spanish: "Esta cuenta tenía cero seguidores en enero. Catorce meses después tiene 277000, y nunca
> usó un reel."
> Portuguese: "Essa conta tinha zero seguidores em janeiro. Catorze meses depois, chegou a 277 mil, e
> nunca usou um reels."

### 3. The Continuation/Twist
Picks up right where the image's line left off, then bends it somewhere the headline didn't go.
Reliable default when nothing else fits, but only if it genuinely adds a new beat, not a rephrase.
> Image headline: "Standards beat *talent*." → Caption: "Here's the part that never makes the
> highlight reel, and it has nothing to do with talent."
> Spanish: "Titular: 'El nivel vence al talento.' → Leyenda: 'Esta es la parte que nunca sale en el
> resumen, y no tiene nada que ver con el talento.'"
> Portuguese: "Título: 'O nível vence o talento.' → Legenda: 'Essa é a parte que nunca aparece no
> resumo, e não tem nada a ver com talento.'"

### 4. The Confession
A blunt, slightly too-honest admission that makes the reader feel like they're getting something
unscripted, then the paragraph explains what changed your mind.
> "I used to think this was a talent problem. It isn't, and the difference matters more than it
> sounds."
> Spanish: "Antes pensaba que esto era un problema de talento. No lo es, y la diferencia importa más
> de lo que parece."
> Portuguese: "Eu achava que isso era um problema de talento. Não é, e a diferença importa mais do que
> parece."

### 5. The Direct Address
Speaks to one specific reader as if this caption was written for them, not broadcast to everyone.
> "If you run ads and you're proud of your CTR, this is going to be an annoying read."
> Spanish: "Si manejas anuncios y estás orgulloso de tu CTR, esto te va a incomodar un poco."
> Portuguese: "Se você roda anúncios e tem orgulho do seu CTR, essa leitura vai te incomodar um
> pouco."

### 6. The Wrong Turn
Opens like it's about to talk about something else entirely, then snaps to the real subject by the
second sentence. Builds a small, fast curiosity gap.
> "Everyone talks about the lap time. Almost nobody talks about the Thursday before it, which is the
> part that actually explains it."
> Spanish: "Todos hablan del tiempo de vuelta. Casi nadie habla del jueves anterior, que es la parte
> que en realidad lo explica."
> Portuguese: "Todo mundo fala do tempo de volta. Quase ninguém fala da véspera, que é a parte que
> realmente explica isso." (Note: avoid words like "quinta-feira" that require a mandatory hyphen in
> Portuguese orthography; reword around them the same way a headline reworks around an English hyphen.)

### 7. The Genuine Question
Not a rhetorical "ever wonder why," but a specific question tied to the reader's actual situation, one
they could genuinely answer, opening straight into the paragraph that answers it.
> "What's the number in your business you've quietly stopped promising. There's usually a reason, and
> it's rarely the one you'd say out loud."
> Spanish: "Cuál es el número en tu negocio que dejaste de prometer en silencio. Casi siempre hay una
> razón, y casi nunca es la que dirías en voz alta."
> Portuguese: "Qual é o número no seu negócio que você parou de prometer em silêncio. Quase sempre tem
> um motivo, e quase nunca é o que você diria em voz alta."

### 8. The Single-Word or Two-Word Punch (rare — an occasional device, not the default shape)
One short line, alone, before the paragraph that explains it. Real examples don't open this way; use
it sparingly, mid-caption, as a structural break inside an otherwise paragraph-first caption, not as
the caption's whole rhythm.
> "Untouchable.
>
> That's what they called him, and at first it wasn't a compliment."

**Craft check for every opening, regardless of technique:** copy just the first ~125 characters into a
blank line by themselves. If that fragment alone doesn't already pull, the technique is right but the
execution isn't. Rewrite before moving on. That's roughly where Instagram's feed view cuts the caption
before "... more."

## 2. Structure: three paragraphs, not fragments

Default shape, matching the real account (section 0):

- **Paragraph 1** — the opening move (one of the eight techniques above), grounded in something
  concrete, often a real number or a real, current detail. Two to four sentences.
- **Paragraph 2** — the mechanism, the development, or the turn. This is usually where a contrast or a
  reveal lives. Two to four sentences.
- **Paragraph 3** — the takeaway, landed as a genuine final sentence, not a summary of paragraphs 1
  and 2. A summary reads like a lecture ending; a real final turn reads like an idea landing.
- **CTA** — its own short line or two, separated by a blank line from paragraph 3 (see section 5). Some
  of the real reference's strongest captions skip the CTA entirely (Example C) when the final sentence
  of paragraph 3 already lands hard enough on its own; that's a legitimate choice here too, not a gap.

Blank line between each paragraph so it still reads on a phone. Vary sentence length inside each
paragraph, not just across the whole caption. A one- or two-word fragment line (technique 8) is a tool
for an occasional emphasis beat, not the caption's default rhythm.

**Freshness check, every time:** could this exact caption have been posted a year ago, unchanged, or
five years from now? If yes, it's not anchored in anything current and it will read generic no matter
how well it's written. Give it something that could only be true right now: a current number from the
brief, a current platform or culture behavior, a real detail from a quick live check if the brief
doesn't already carry one. Examples A and B both do this explicitly (a live algorithm shift, a live
flood of AI-generic content) and it's a real part of why they don't read like stock content.

## 3. Slide voice vs. caption voice — what changes when there's room to breathe

The slide headline gets three to nine words. The caption gets paragraphs. That is the real difference,
not a difference in how serious the writing is allowed to be. The real examples in section 0 are dense,
well-built, adult sentences: this is not a "dumb it down" or "make it casual" exercise, and it is not a
text-message exercise either.

**Still true in both the slide copy and the caption (universal AI-tells, not a register choice):**
- No banned binary constructions, no AI clichés, no vague-data phrases, no essay-opener
  throat-clearing (full list in section 4).
- Every noun keeps its article; a dropped article is the single most common AI tell.
- Read it aloud. If it sounds translated, or like a bullet list wearing a sentence's clothes, rewrite
  it as a real paragraph.

**Genuinely different in the caption, because there's room:**
- The idea can develop across three paragraphs instead of compressing into one line.
- Direct address and first person are fair game ("you," "I used to think," "here's what changes") in a
  way the on-image decode copy generally avoids.
- The caption can name the mechanism outright instead of just gesturing at it, because it has the
  space to actually explain instead of only claim.
- In English, contractions read more natural and are expected where they fit ("it's," "doesn't,"
  "won't") — this doesn't show up in the Portuguese reference examples in section 0 because Spanish and
  Portuguese don't contract the same way English does; it's an English-specific craft note, not a voice
  change. When writing Rarity's own Portuguese caption, lean instead on the natural informal contractions
  Brazilian Portuguese does have in speech and casual writing ("pra" for "para," "tá" for "está," used
  sparingly and only where the register genuinely calls for it) rather than forcing formal, uncontracted
  Portuguese throughout — that reads stiffer than the reference account ever does.

## 4. The human voice test

Run every caption, EN, ES, and PT, through this before it ships. Any "yes" means rewrite that specific
line, not the whole caption — most captions only need one or two lines fixed.

1. **Would a sharp, opinionated writer actually put their name on this sentence?** Or does it sound
   like committee copy, softened until it means nothing? If it reads like copy, it is copy.
2. **Does the opening survive alone, cut at ~125 characters?** Paste just that fragment on its own line
   and read it isolated from the rest.
3. **Is there a banned construction hiding in it?** Scan against the table below.
4. **Does the sentence length actually vary inside each paragraph, or is every sentence the same
   shape?** Uniform sentence length is the fastest tell that a line was drafted rather than argued.
5. **Could this exact caption, unchanged, sit under a different post about a different subject, or
   have run a year ago or five years from now?** If yes, it's generic and it's not current. Both are
   failures (see section 2's freshness check).

### Banned constructions (reject on sight, rewrite, never just delete)

| Banned | Do instead |
|---|---|
| "It's not X, it's Y" | Show the actual difference without naming the formula |
| "And that changes everything" | Say specifically what changes |
| "At the end of the day" | Cut it, or make the point directly |
| "Here's the thing:" / "The truth is:" | Just make the point |
| "In a world where..." | Start on the fact |
| "Studies show..." / "Experts say..." / "Most people..." | Cite the brief's actual number and source, or cut the claim |
| "Simply" / "Basically" / "Just" | Cut |
| "Hope you enjoyed this!" / "Thanks for reading" / "Don't forget to follow" | Replace with a directive save/share close (section 5) |
| "Swipe to see more →" as a caption line | That belongs on the slide, not the caption |
| Any hashtag or emoji | Never. See Output discipline in SKILL.md. |

**A real tension worth naming:** Example C's real caption ends "Contentamento não é acomodação: é
recuperar a atenção..." which is structurally "it's not X, it's Y," the very first banned construction
above. A strong individual writer can occasionally earn that move. A skill generating captions at
scale, on demand, cannot reliably tell the earned version from the lazy one, so the ban stays as
Rarity's safety rail rather than a rule to bend because the reference sometimes does.

## 5. The CTA bank

Same intent every time (save it, share it with a specific person), different sentence. Directive, not
grateful. Pick one, or write a new one in the same family, or skip the CTA entirely when paragraph
three's final sentence already lands hard enough on its own (see section 2 and Example C). Never reuse
the identical sentence two posts in a row.

**English**
- "Save this. Send it to a founder who needs the reminder."
- "Save this for the next time someone tells you to be realistic."
- "Send this to the one person on your team who'll actually use it."
- "Save it before it disappears into the feed. You'll want it back."
- "Read it again. Then send it to whoever needs to hear it more than you do."
- "Screenshot the number. You'll need it in your next planning meeting."

**Spanish**
- "Guarda esto. Envíaselo a un fundador que necesita el recordatorio."
- "Guarda esto para la próxima vez que alguien te diga que seas realista."
- "Envíaselo a la única persona de tu equipo que de verdad lo va a usar."
- "Guárdalo antes de que se pierda en el feed. Lo vas a necesitar de nuevo."
- "Léelo otra vez. Después envíaselo a quien lo necesite más que tú."
- "Captura el número. Lo vas a necesitar en tu próxima reunión de planeación."

**Portuguese**
- "Salva isso. Manda pra um fundador que precisa desse lembrete."
- "Salva isso pra próxima vez que alguém disser pra você ser realista."
- "Manda pra única pessoa do seu time que realmente vai usar isso."
- "Salva antes que suma no feed. Você vai precisar de novo."
- "Leia de novo. Depois manda pra quem precisa ouvir isso mais do que você."
- "Print esse número. Você vai precisar dele na próxima reunião de planejamento."

No hashtags after the CTA. Ever (see Output discipline in SKILL.md).

## 6. Worked example: Senna, before and after

This is the same brief `rarity-ig-idea-engine` already ships as its own worked example
(`references/brief-template.md`), so it's a fair, checkable comparison.

**On the image:** Headline "Standards beat *talent*." Open loop subhead: "What happens when the whole
garage has to catch up to one driver?"

**The brief's placeholder caption (what Step 1 drafts as a rough first pass):**
> Talent gets you noticed. Standards make you untouchable.
> ...
> Save this. Send it to a founder who needs the reminder.
> #RarityAgency #Senna #HighPerformance

Functional, but it opens by basically re-reading the headline, it's a fragment-line list rather than a
real paragraph, and it carries hashtags. It would pass a spellcheck; it would not pass the human voice
test, and it's no longer the house standard now that hashtags are banned outright.

**This skill's version:**

EN — technique: Blunt Fact Drop, three paragraphs
> Senna qualified 1.4 seconds ahead of his own teammate at Monaco in 1988, in the same car, in the
> same garage, with nothing different between them except the driver. Nobody handed him that number.
> He decided the lap was possible before the stopwatch agreed, then drove until it caught up to him.
>
> Most people call that talent and move on. Look closer and it's a standard: he set the number first
> and made the rest of the team chase it. That distinction is the part every retelling of this story
> tends to skip.
>
> Your brand has its own version of that lap right now, the number you keep rounding down because it
> feels safer to promise less. Stop rounding it down.
>
> Save this for the next time someone tells you to be realistic. Send it to a founder who needs it.

ES — same technique, rebuilt, not translated:
> Senna sacó 1.4 segundos de ventaja a su propio compañero de equipo en Mónaco, 1988, en el mismo
> auto, en el mismo garaje, sin nada distinto entre ellos salvo el piloto. Nadie le regaló ese número.
> Decidió que la vuelta era posible antes de que el cronómetro le diera la razón, y manejó hasta que lo
> alcanzó.
>
> La mayoría llama a eso talento y sigue de largo. Mira más de cerca y es un estándar: puso el número
> primero e hizo que todo el equipo lo persiguiera. Esa distinción es la parte que casi nunca se cuenta.
>
> Tu marca tiene su propia vuelta de Mónaco ahora mismo, el número que sigues redondeando hacia abajo
> porque parece más seguro prometer menos. Deja de redondearlo.
>
> Guarda esto para la próxima vez que alguien te diga que seas realista. Envíaselo a un fundador que lo
> necesita.

PT — same technique, rebuilt for a Brazilian reader, not translated from either version above:
> Senna tirou 1,4 segundo de vantagem do próprio companheiro de equipe em Mônaco, 1988, no mesmo carro,
> na mesma equipe, sem nada diferente entre eles além do piloto. Ninguém deu esse número de bandeja.
> Ele decidiu que a volta era possível antes de o cronômetro concordar, e dirigiu até o tempo alcançar
> ele.
>
> A maioria chama isso de talento e segue em frente. Olhando de perto, é um padrão: ele colocou o
> número primeiro e fez o resto da equipe correr atrás. Essa diferença é a parte que quase nenhuma
> versão dessa história conta.
>
> Sua marca tem a própria volta de Mônaco agora, o número que você segue arredondando pra baixo porque
> parece mais seguro prometer menos. Para de arredondar.
>
> Salva isso pra próxima vez que alguém disser pra você ser realista. Manda pra um fundador que precisa
> disso.

**What changed and why:** the caption opens on the real fact, not the on-image headline. It reads as
three built paragraphs, not a list of short fragments, matching what the real reference actually does.
No hashtags anywhere. Sentence length still swings inside each paragraph, contractions carry the
English version's voice (and Brazilian Portuguese's own natural informal contractions carry the PT
version's), and the CTA keeps the save-and-share intent without repeating the exact sentence used in
the brief's own placeholder.
